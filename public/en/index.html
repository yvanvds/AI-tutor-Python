For students

Learn Python with your own tutor

The AI Tutor is a Python tutor that reads along with you, asks questions at your level and helps you along without giving away the answer.

Download for Windows How it works
In the app

How it works

At the top you choose between Explain, Practice and Playground. In Explain and Practice, the tutor is right beside you, in the chat.

Explain

You read the lesson of the subgoal you are working on. You run the code examples yourself with Run.

Don't get something? Ask your question in the chat. The tutor knows which lesson you are reading.

With Previous you page back to lessons you have already read, from earlier goals too. Done reading? Try it yourself takes you to Practice.

Practice

The tutor gives you exercises: writing code, completing code, explaining what code does, predicting what it prints, or an open question. You test your code with Run and send it to the tutor, who tells you what is already right and what isn't yet.

Stuck? Click the light bulb or type ? for a hint. The tutor helps you on your way, but won't give away the solution, not even if you ask for it.

Top right you see how hard the exercise is: easy, medium or hard.

Quiz questions

Every now and then you get a quiz question: a multiple-choice question that takes the place of the editor for a moment. You click an answer and see straight away whether it is right, and why.

Not sure? Ask in the chat. The tutor explains the question, but doesn't give away the answer.

Playground

Free coding, without a goal and without the tutor: just you and Python.

Keep your code with Save and open it again later with Open. Your files are stored with your account, so you can find them on another computer too.

At your level

The tutor adapts to you

Not everyone gets the same questions. What you get depends on what you can already do.

The app knows what you can already do

Every subgoal has a few learning objectives: small things you need to be able to do, like predicting which branch of an if-else runs. After every exercise, the app estimates again how well you can do each learning objective.

Your next exercise is usually about the learning objective you are weakest at.

Easier or harder

You practise at one of three difficulty levels: easy, medium or hard. Everyone starts at medium.

If things go well for a while, your questions get harder. If things go less well for a while, they get easier.

Only done when you really know it

A subgoal is done when you have demonstrated all of its learning objectives. Optional learning objectives aren't required. One right answer is not enough: you need at least two, and at least one of them must not be a quiz question.

If a learning objective really doesn't work out, the tutor moves on after a while anyway. It comes back later.

Old material comes back

What you could already do comes back again later. Sometimes a session starts with a warm-up question about something from an earlier subgoal.

A learning objective that didn't quite work out yet comes back later, as a check question.

How does the tutor decide that? You can read it in detail on the page for teachers.

Your progress

You see how far you've come

The app shows what you can already do and what you can't do yet. That way you know what to work on next.

Learning path

All your goals in a row. For every subgoal you see how many learning objectives you have already demonstrated, for example 2 of 3 demonstrated. Open it up and you see which ones are still to do.

With Finish you complete an earlier subgoal that didn't quite work out yet. With Review you practise one again. Continue takes you back to your current goal.

While you practise, the bar under Current goal shows one piece per learning objective. A half piece means: one more right answer.

XP and levels

Every exercise earns you 20 XP, even if your answer was wrong. What you demonstrate earns extra XP: 100 per subgoal.

Every 500 XP takes you up a level. Your level is shown top right in the app.

Trophy case

Badges for what you do: for effort, for right answers in a row, for code you wrote yourself. Some are secret. Others you get from your teacher, for things the app can't see.

Do you finish a subgoal as one of the first three in your class? Then you get gold, silver or bronze on the Class podium. Only you see your medals.

Badges give no XP and don't count towards your grade.

My reports

At report time, your teacher releases your report. Under My reports you read your grade and why you got it.

Want to check it? Below your report you see how your grade was calculated, and the rules are under Grade formula.

The course

What will you learn?

The course is made up of goals, and every goal of subgoals. You work through them in this order. Click a goal to see its subgoals.

  1. 1 Writing and understanding simple Python scripts 5 subgoals

    You can write short Python scripts that output numbers and text, use variables to store values, do simple calculations with the standard operators, and process input from the user.

    Output with print
    You can use print() to show strings and numbers on the screen, and you recognize the difference between what is in the source code and what appears as output.
    Variables
    You can assign a value to a variable with =, choose a valid name, use the variable in expressions and in print, and reassign its value.
    Calculating with numbers
    You can write and evaluate arithmetic expressions with the standard operators (+, -, *, /, //, %, **), apply the order of operations, and recognize the difference between int and float.
    Input with input
    You can use input() to ask the user for a value with a clear prompt, store the result in a variable, and convert the input to int or float when you need to calculate with it.
    Putting together a small script
    You can combine the building blocks covered so far — input, variables, calculations, print — into a small, working Python script that solves a problem described in plain language, and you can read, trace and debug such a script.
  2. 2 Programming decisions with conditions 4 subgoals

    You can write comparisons that give True or False, program decisions with if, elif and else, combine conditions with and, or and not, and use the turtle module to make drawings in which conditions decide what gets drawn.

    Comparisons and booleans
    You can use comparison operators (==, !=, <, >, <=, >=) to write expressions that give True or False, store that result in a variable, and predict what a comparison gives.
    Decisions with if and else
    You can write an if statement that runs code when a condition is true, add an else branch to choose between two blocks, and predict which branch runs.
    More choices with elif, and, or and not
    You can tell several cases apart with an if/elif/else chain and combine conditions with and, or and not.
    Drawing with turtle
    You can program a drawing with the turtle module: create a turtle, move and turn it, set the pen and the color, and use conditions to decide what gets drawn.
  3. 3 Programming repetition with loops 4 subgoals

    You can implement repetition with for and while, test and debug programs systematically with different test cases, and translate more complex problems into working Python code, including turtle drawings.

    Repeating with for and range
    You can write a for loop with range to repeat an operation a fixed number of times, predict the output of such a loop, build up a result in a variable, and draw regular shapes with turtle.
    Repeating with while
    You can write a while loop that repeats as long as a condition is true, predict when such a loop stops, recognize infinite loops, and use a loop to validate input.
    Testing and debugging
    You can test a program systematically by trying out different test cases, choose meaningful test cases including edge cases, interpret error messages, and find and fix errors.
    From problem to program
    You can break a more complex problem down into steps and translate it into a working Python program that combines conditions, loops and possibly turtle, and find and fix logic errors in it.
  4. 4 Lists and tuples 6 subgoals

    You can store data in lists and tuples: get and change elements by their index or with slicing, loop over lists and strings, write classic list algorithms yourself, make use of the difference between mutable lists and immutable tuples, and work with nested loops on pairs and grids.

    Lists, strings and indexes
    You can create a list, get and change elements by their index (including a negative one), take out a part with slicing, and index a string the same way, as a sequence of characters.
    Changing lists with methods
    You can make a list grow and shrink with append, insert, remove and pop, check with in whether a value is in it, and use sum, min, max and sort.
    Looping over lists and strings
    You can use a for loop to process every element of a list or string, choose between a loop over the elements and a loop over the indexes, build a new list by filtering or transforming, and draw with turtle based on a list.
    Classic list patterns
    You can write common algorithms over a list yourself: find the largest or smallest element without max() or min(), count what meets a condition, search whether and where a value occurs, and swap two elements.
    Tuples and immutability
    You can create, index and unpack tuples (also to swap values or to loop over a list of pairs), explain why tuples and strings can't be changed, and predict what happens when two names refer to the same list.
    Nested loops and lists of lists
    You can write a loop inside a loop to go through pairs from a list, predict how often the inner loop runs, and loop through and change a grid as a list of lists with grid[r][c].
  5. 5 Functions 6 subgoals

    You can write and call your own functions: give code a name with def, pass data in through parameters, give back a result with return, predict which variables exist inside and outside a function, translate formulas from math, physics and economics into functions and make tables of values with them, and split a bigger problem into functions that call each other.

    Writing and calling a function
    You can use def to write a function that takes in a value through a parameter and gives back a result with return, call it, and store the result, print it or use it further in an expression.
    Parameters and arguments
    You can give a function several parameters, call it with the right arguments in the right order, predict which value each parameter gets, and generalize a calculation you need more than once into a function.
    A closer look at return
    You can predict what goes wrong when a function prints instead of giving back a value and fix that, predict that a function stops at the first return, write functions that give back True or False, and recognize when a function does something, such as drawing, instead of calculating something.
    Local variables
    You can predict which variables exist inside and outside a function, what happens to a variable from outside when the function assigns a value to a variable with the same name, and explain why a function should get its data through parameters and give it back through return.
    Mathematical functions and tables of values
    You can translate a rule from math, physics or economics into a Python function, use the math module, make a table of values with a loop, and work out something about the function from those values, such as where it is smallest or changes sign.
    Splitting a problem into functions
    You can split a bigger problem into functions that each have one task, write functions that call other functions or work with lists, and test a function with well-chosen calls.
  6. 6 Randomness and simulation 5 subgoals

    You can bring randomness into a program with the random module and use it to act out experiments: make random values, estimate a probability by repeating an experiment many times, estimate an area with random points, program a random walk, and translate a described situation into a simulation that you check with a case whose answer you know.

    Making random numbers
    You can make random values with the random module: a whole number between two limits, a decimal number between 0 and 1 or a random element from a list, make an event happen with a given probability, and predict which output of such a script is possible.
    Estimating a probability by repeating
    You can repeat a random experiment many times, count how often an event occurs and so estimate its probability, keep track of how often each outcome comes up, and explain why more repetitions give a more reliable estimate.
    Estimating an area with random points
    You can make random points inside a rectangle, check whether a point is inside a shape, and estimate the area of that shape from the share of hits, such as a circle or the area under a graph.
    Random walks
    You can program a random walk in which a position changes randomly at every step, keep the path in a list, make a walk stop at a limit, and explain what many repeated walks show that one walk does not.
    From situation to simulation
    You can translate a described situation with randomness into a simulation: decide what one experiment is, write that experiment as a function, repeat it many times to estimate a probability or an average, and check the simulation with a case whose answer you know.
The app

Everything's included

You install one app, and you can get started.

Python included

You don't need to install Python, nor turtle, matplotlib, numpy or pandas. They are in the app. Your code runs on your own laptop.

Dutch or English

The app, the lessons and what the tutor writes are available in Dutch and in English. You choose your language in Options → Language.

Dark or light

The app follows the Windows theme. In Options → Appearance you choose Light or Dark yourself.

Updates itself

When there is a new version, you see a bar at the top of the app. Click Update and you carry on in the new version.

Installing the app

For now only for SMA Arcadia The app currently only works for students of SMA Arcadia. You sign in with your school account. A version without a school account is in preparation (#261 on GitHub).

Download for Windows

Version, date, size and SHA-256: see all releases on GitHub.

Installing, step by step

  1. Download the installer

    Click Download for Windows. Does your browser ask whether you want to keep the file? Then choose Keep.

  2. Start the installer

    Open python_teacher_install.exe from your Downloads folder. Does Windows show a blue SmartScreen window? Click More info and then Run anyway. Windows warns you because the app is new and has no digital signature.

  3. Install it just for you

    Choose Install for me only and click Next until you see Install. You don't need admin rights: the app is installed for you only.

  4. Start the app

    Leave Launch Python Teacher ticked and click Finish. The app starts right away. Later you'll find it in the Start menu as Python Teacher.

  5. Sign in

    Click Sign in with school account and sign in with your school Microsoft account.

    Does the app then ask for an API key? Then your teacher still has to switch your account on. You don't need a key yourself. Tell your teacher and leave the app open: as soon as your account is on, you carry on by yourself.

What you need

Windows 10 or 11, 64-bit. And internet: the tutor and your progress are online.

You don't need to install Python. It is in the app, with turtle, matplotlib, numpy and pandas.

Updates

The app updates itself. When there is a new version, you see a bar at the top of the app. Click Update: the app closes for a moment and comes back in the new version. You don't need to come back to this site.

Want to check yourself? Go to Options → About and click Check for updates.

Reporting a problem

Go to Options → Bug reports, click Report a bug… and write what went wrong. Click Save as file and send that file to your teacher.

Have a GitHub account? Then click Connect GitHub first. After that, Post on GitHub puts your report straight on GitHub.
