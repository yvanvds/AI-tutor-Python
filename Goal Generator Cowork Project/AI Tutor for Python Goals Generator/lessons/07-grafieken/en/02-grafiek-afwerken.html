A line without explanation is a riddle: what is on the axes, and in which unit? A graph is only finished when someone else can read it without seeing your code.

Title and axis labels

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

years = [0, 1, 2, 3, 4]
balance = [1000, 1020, 1040, 1061, 1082]
plt.plot(years, balance)
plt.title("Savings account with 2 percent interest")
plt.xlabel("time (years)")
plt.ylabel("balance (euros)")
plt.show()

plt.title puts a title above the graph, plt.xlabel a text below the horizontal axis and plt.ylabel a text next to the vertical axis. Put the quantity and the unit in an axis label: "balance (euros)" says more than "balance".

Several lines and a legend

months = [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6]
costs = [500, 600, 700, 800, 900, 1000]
revenue = [0, 225, 450, 675, 900, 1125]
plt.plot(months, costs, label="costs")
plt.plot(months, revenue, label="revenue")
plt.xlabel("month")
plt.ylabel("amount (euros)")
plt.legend()
plt.show()

Every call of plt.plot adds a line to the same figure: two lines here, each in its own color. With label="costs" you give a line a name, and plt.legend() shows those names in a small box. If you forget plt.legend(), you see the names nowhere.

label="costs" is a named argument: you pass it with its name in front, instead of by order. Named arguments come after the ordinary ones. That way you choose exactly which setting you want to change and leave the others at their default value.

Color and grid

plt.plot(months, costs, label="costs", color="red")
plt.plot(months, revenue, label="revenue", color="green")
plt.legend()
plt.grid()
plt.show()

With color= you choose the color yourself, with an English name such as "red", "blue", "green", "orange" or "black". plt.grid() draws a grid, on which you read values more easily: the two lines cross at month 5.

Everything before plt.show()

plt.plot(years, balance)
plt.show()
plt.title("Too late")

plt.show() shows the figure as it is at that moment and waits until you close the window. What comes after plt.show() no longer ends up in that figure: this graph has no title.

What does a reader need?

Imagine someone who only sees the window. Two lines and numbers on the axes don't tell what was measured. The reader has to be able to find three things: what the graph is about (the title), which quantity in which unit is on each axis (the axis labels), and which line stands for what (the legend, as soon as there is more than one line). Color and grid help, but without those three a graph stays a drawing.

With a title, two axis labels and a legend your graph tells its story itself.
